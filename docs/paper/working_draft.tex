\begin{abstract}
We document a controlled simulation protocol for collecting labeled robot
grasp attempts from Human Universal Grasping (HUG) proposals. Fresh calibrated
RGB-D inputs, content-bound geometry and scene records, a fixed actuated Shadow
Hand controller, and bounded episode-level finger-target disturbances make the
collection inspectable and repeatable. The initial collection contains 1,040
attempts from two geometries, four observation families, and sixteen fresh
proposals: 444 task successes, 596 task failures, and no validation or runtime
errors. Requested disturbances frequently encounter joint limits, so requested
noise, applied target offsets, and measured motion are saved separately. Task
labels and conservative sampled event annotations are also separated: missing
a lift cutoff is not evidence of a physical slip. A regularized logistic model
using pre-lift joint-state and command summaries attains failure AUROC 0.8523,
log loss 0.3771, and Brier score 0.1170 on the held-out apple observation. At a
fixed threshold it flags 187 of 202 failures and 22 of 58 successes. These are
retrospective development results from one test observation, not calibrated
deployment rates. This draft does not establish slip-onset prediction, useful
warning lead time, recovery, or real-world transfer.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Grasp generation and successful physical execution are different problems.
A proposed pose can be plausible while the executed hand fails to acquire the
object or retain it during lifting. Our longer-term question is whether the
robot's observations and actions can warn of a forthcoming failure early enough
for a useful intervention. Before testing that question, we need a reproducible
execution system, a defensible prediction target, and trajectories whose input
and label provenance can be checked.

This pilot uses HUG to supply grasp hypotheses and a fixed controller to execute
them. It varies finger targets while holding the execution protocol fixed.
The current deliverable is an audited collection protocol and a first pre-lift
task-outcome baseline. Its practical research questions are whether we can
preserve source scenes, applied commands, and label semantics while collecting
varied attempts, and whether measured closing response adds predictive
information beyond the commanded target history.

The completed work comprises fresh observation capture and proposal inference;
geometry, camera, and scene-content validation; paired bounded disturbances;
unchanged binary task scoring; conservative event annotations; replay and
collection audits; a validated causal-prefix export with observation-disjoint
development splits; and a compact trained baseline with a command-only
comparison. The resulting 1,040 attempts belong to only four observation
families. This distinction is central to subsequent evaluation: repeating a
proposal with new disturbances does not create an independent object or scene.

\section{Related work and design implications}
\paragraph{Grasp hypotheses and benchmark assets.}
HUG predicts wrist placement and a MANO hand pose from a selected object in an
RGB-D image, with retargeting to robot embodiments. Its released benchmark
provides metric object meshes and geometric categories \cite{hug}. We use fresh
HUG proposals and one reviewed development asset, rather than official benchmark
grasp labels. Our Shadow Hand execution and task scorer are project-specific;
the reported collection is not an official HUG-Bench score.

\paragraph{Outcome prediction and sensing.}
Calandra et al. compare vision, touch, and their combination for grasp-outcome
prediction using a two-finger gripper with GelSight sensors \cite{feeling}.
This motivates later information-source ablations, but does not establish that
our simulator contacts are equivalent to tactile measurements. The initial
predictor should use a documented subset of robot-observable signals before
adding any contact or visual features.

\paragraph{Current slip, future dynamics, and anomaly monitoring.}
SlipSense uses causal histories of pressure and vibration signals to classify
current contact/slip states and evaluates detection latency and false alarms
\cite{slipsense}. This motivates keeping current-slip detection distinct from
future object loss and measuring warning time at a controlled false-alarm budget.
FeelWorld predicts action-conditioned visual and tactile states with separate
contact and slip supervision \cite{feelworld}; it motivates preserving action
history and deferring a larger world model until a simpler target is understood.
Ward et al. use probabilistic world-model uncertainty for conformal runtime
monitoring \cite{ward}. Their trajectory-level calibration discussion motivates
grouped evaluation and caution about guarantees under distribution shift. None
of these systems is reproduced in this pilot.

\paragraph{Simulation.}
MuJoCo provides articulated dynamics, actuators, and contact simulation
\cite{mujoco}. Its utility for controlled execution does not by itself establish
realistic friction or slip on physical hardware. We report simulator-derived
labels and same-runtime replay evidence separately from any future transfer
claim.

\section{Collection methods}
\subsection{Frozen embodiment and execution}
The experiment resolves the versioned environment
\path{adroit_shadow_tabletop_v2}, embodiment \path{shadow_hand_right}, and
protocol \path{fixed_grasp_lift_v2}. HUG's landmarks and wrist transform are
converted from the calibrated camera frame and retargeted with the supplied
right Shadow Hand configuration in Dex Retargeting \cite{dex}. Joint outputs
are mapped by name and checked before execution.

The fixed sequence performs pregrasp, approach, finger closing, settling,
lifting, holding, and scoring. The high-level controller does not replan in
response to a disturbance or outcome; its low-level position servos remain
force-limited and respond to contact. The commanded lift is 0.15 m. Physics
steps are 2 ms and saved control intervals are 40 ms. This pilot evaluates
grasp--lift--hold, not transport, placement, or recovery.

\subsection{Objects, observations, and provenance}
We use the existing 0.18 kg cube and a 0.2304 kg apple from the HUG development
asset \path{val/medium_3/apple}. Each geometry has two requested object-position
and camera scenes, producing four unique initial observation identities. HUG
inference seeds 0--3 produce four fresh proposals per scene, for sixteen proposal
families. Initial images are 224-by-224 RGB with registered metric depth, an
object mask, camera intrinsics, and a camera-to-world transform. Inference uses
50 CPU integration steps from a pinned released checkpoint; no HUG weights are
trained here.

Capture uses the geometry that will be executed and records its actual settled
pose and mass. A versioned scene contract binds object identity, the geometry
dependency closure, pose, mass, environment configuration, calibration, and
all six observation-file hashes. The original contract is copied into each
proposal directory. Validation additionally checks inference output, reports,
manifest bytes, selected pixel, seed, and source/software provenance before
labeling an attempt. Content binding detects mismatches; it cannot prove an
arbitrary external capture was truthful.

The apple's upright starting setup rolled off the table and was rejected before
grasp generation. Inverted setups remained geometrically stationary but
showed small instantaneous contact jitter. Capture therefore requires a full
one-second window with positional bounding-box diagonal at most 0.1 mm,
conservative angular diameter at most 0.002 rad, continuous table contact, no
hand contact, and an object center inside the working table. It must finish
within 2,000 physics steps (4 s). The upright negative control still fails.
The apple templates request a 180-degree X rotation and center height
0.04295394025198684 m, 0.1 mm above the transformed collision bounds. Actual
captured pose and velocities are saved; rollout reset uses that pose with the
protocol's standard zero initial velocities. No mesh, mass, friction, or in-run
velocity changes were used to force settling.

\subsection{Bounded target disturbances}
For proposal $p$ and repetition $r$, draw one vector
$\boldsymbol\xi_{p,r}$ whose finger components are independently uniform on
$[-1,1]$; both wrist components are zero. For amplitude $a$ radians, the requested
episode bias is $\boldsymbol\delta=a\boldsymbol\xi_{p,r}$. In the designated
closing, lift, hold, and score phases, a commanded target is
\begin{equation}
 \mathbf u_t=\operatorname{clip}_{[\mathbf l,\mathbf h]}
                  (\mathbf u_t^{\mathrm{nom}}+\boldsymbol\delta),
 \qquad
 \boldsymbol\delta_t^{\mathrm{applied}}
                  =\mathbf u_t-\mathbf u_t^{\mathrm{nom}}.
\end{equation}
There are 22 perturbed finger targets and two unperturbed wrist targets. One
bias remains fixed within an episode; this is not fresh random noise at every
frame, observation noise, an inserted obstacle, or a measured hardware-error
model. The actuator interpolates targets at physics substeps, and measured
joint motion can differ from both the nominal and disturbed targets.

The schedule uses $a\in\{0,0.15,0.6\}$ rad. Nonzero amplitudes share the same
draw for a proposal/repetition, providing 512 paired directions. Each proposal
has one deterministic zero-noise baseline and 32 repetitions at each nonzero
amplitude. Thus $16(1+32+32)=1,040$ attempts. Master seed 9400, source hashes,
manifest ordering, and per-attempt seeds are frozen in the collection plan.
Requested and clipped offsets are saved separately.

\subsection{Unchanged task labels}
For each completed physical attempt, label $y=1$ if the registered scorer's
conditions hold and $y=0$ otherwise. Invalid inputs and runtime errors have a
separate error status with a null label. Let $z_0$ be the first recorded object
center height. Success requires qualifying hand--object contact during
closing/lifting/holding, peak lift at least 0.14 m above $z_0$, at least 2 s of
consecutive HOLD samples above $z_0+0.12$ m, and peak-to-final height loss at
most 0.02 m. It also requires no hand--table collision during pregrasp/approach
and no protocol timeout. Hold duration follows the scorer's sample-count-times-
control-interval convention; continuous between-sample retention is not proved.

The first applicable failure category is recorded in order: timeout, approach
collision, missing qualifying contact, failed lift acquisition, final drop,
then insufficient hold. The scorer's contact-based \path{acquired_object}
field alone does not establish a secured grip. These labels express a frozen
task definition, not physical slip mechanisms.

\subsection{Conservative temporal annotations and timing}
Separately versioned annotations record a lifted-contact proxy, a retained-lift
proxy, an acquisition deadline, and a sampled height-loss interval. The lift
proxy requires the lift threshold, hand--object contact, and no table contact;
the retention proxy adds the required sampled HOLD duration. The acquisition
deadline is the observed interval from the last LIFT endpoint to the first HOLD
endpoint. Missing acquisition by that deadline is not a slip beginning there.
Post-proxy height loss is bounded by consecutive sampled timestamps; its
physical mechanism remains unknown. Missing references, gaps, incomplete
follow-up, and unsupported causes remain explicit. Events never overwrite the
binary task outcome.

Joint and object states use control-interval endpoint times. MuJoCo body/contact
and force diagnostics precede the final physics integration substep and are
normally 2 ms earlier; this offset is logged. Neither 40 ms sampling nor a
height crossing supplies exact slip-onset ground truth. The event files are
supervision/analysis metadata, not predictor inputs.

\subsection{Saved artifacts and audits}
The plan, index, and summary describe the collection. Each episode stores nominal
and disturbed commands, requested/applied offsets, joint state, object state,
contact diagnostics, scores, events, resolved configurations, runtime metadata,
binary scene, and simulator integration snapshots. Only initial RGB-D images
are captured. Six representative videos were rendered later from saved
trajectories; they are not additional attempts or online camera streams.

All 1,040 scores and event files regenerate exactly. The audit checks joint
bounds, clipping, wrist exclusion, phases, timing, RNG records, paired draws,
and all sixteen source bindings. Seven representative complete executions
reproduced states, contacts, and forces bitwise on the same runtime. This is
limited reproducibility evidence, not cross-platform or real-robot validation.

At least one target was clipped in 508 of 512 attempts at 0.15 rad and all 512
at 0.6 rad. The requested amplitude therefore does not describe an equal applied
offset at every joint. All eight apple baselines failed, while 108 disturbed
apple attempts succeeded. A disturbance can raise or lower task success; this
alone does not establish better grip security. Aggregate counts are descriptive; this pilot does not establish
a general monotonic relationship between noise and success or estimate an
independent-population confidence interval.

The 596 failures comprise 590 \path{failed_acquisition}, two
\path{no_contact}, and four \path{drop} categories. The no-contact category
means missing qualifying contact during acquisition phases. There are only
four task-scored drops, so the collection is dominated by acquisition failures
and is not yet a convincing future-loss benchmark.

The cutoff is sensitive: 21 attempts fall within 0.5 mm of 14 cm, comprising
14 successes and seven failures. Apple \path{episode_000465} meets the sampled
hold requirement but peaks only 0.0308 mm below the lift cutoff; its stored
label remains failed acquisition. Thirty further attempts lose more than 2 cm
from peak to final height yet remain failed acquisition because the lift cutoff
was not reached first. Thus category precedence hides some observed height
losses, and a near-cutoff label does not demonstrate grip insecurity. Any
alternative target must receive a new explicit version while retaining these
original outcomes.

\section{Completed dataset export and pre-lift baseline}
\subsection{Exploratory grouped splits}
The collection records three content-derived identities. A proposal group keeps
all noisy variants of one proposal together. An observation group keeps
proposals sharing captured pose/camera/input bytes together. A geometry group
keeps the same geometry closure together regardless of object naming.
Repeated byte-identical captures across preparation batches count as the same
observation. Complete trajectories and every derived window must remain in one
split; frame-wise random splitting would leak shared attempts and sources.

The saved protocol is a retrospective observation-disjoint split:
scenes 0 and 2 train on 520 attempts (231 successes/289 failures), scene 1
validates on 260 cube attempts (155/105), and scene 3 tests on 260 apple attempts
(58/202). Training contains both geometries. Validation contains only cube and
test only apple, so their condition distributions differ. The intended claim is
performance on a held-out apple observation under the frozen task, not an unseen
geometry or a broad unseen-object population. The four scene families provide
limited evidence even when every attempt has a separate trajectory.

The completed \path{task_prefix_dataset_v1} export stores 1,040 rows with
1,440 float32 input columns, each a flattened $20\times72$ causal closing
window. Outcomes and identities are stored separately, outside the model
matrix. Training has eight proposal/two observation families; validation and
test each have four proposal/one observation family. There are no excluded
attempts. The saved feature manifest and split record bind payload hashes,
named column ordering, source-label/scorer agreement, provenance, and memberships.
The actual export passed the validated loader, and 28 focused tests cover
causal/privileged-data mutation, timestamps, groups, corrupt payloads, exclusions,
and overwrite protection. This completes export and splitting for this pilot.

Training fits parameters; validation selects regularization; test is scored
after selection. Although assignments depend on declared scene identities
without using outcomes, this split was chosen after collection and outcome
inspection. It is not a preregistered or untouched final benchmark.

\subsection{Causal first model, then a future-event target}
The first learning task predicts eventual frozen task success from a
prefix ending at a declared time $t$:
\begin{equation}
 \widehat p_t=P_{\theta}\!\left(y=1\mid
       \{\mathbf q_s,\dot{\mathbf q}_s,\mathbf u_s\}_{s\leq t}\right).
\end{equation}
The chosen prefix comprises the last twenty CLOSE\_FINGERS samples, ending at
2.96 s, before the first LIFT sample at 3.00 s. Each row whitelists 24 measured
hand joint positions, 24 joint velocities, and 24 actual commanded targets,
forming a $20\times72$ window. The baseline summarizes each column by its last
value, mean, population standard deviation, and end-minus-start change: 288 features.
A command-only ablation uses the same summaries for 24 command columns, giving
96 features; a constant baseline uses training-set outcome prevalence.

The classifier is L2-regularized logistic regression, optimized with
SciPy L-BFGS-B. Its objective is mean binary cross entropy plus
$\lambda\|\mathbf w\|^2/2$, with an unpenalized intercept. The scaler is fitted
only on training data. Features with training standard deviation at most
$10^{-8}$ are excluded, leaving 269 active features for the full model and 77
for commands only. The fixed candidate grid is $\{0.001,0.01,0.1,1\}$;
minimum validation log loss among converged fits selects $\lambda=0.1$ for both
models. Both selected fits converge. The decision threshold is fixed at
$p_{\mathrm{failure}}\geq0.5$, and test is scored only after regularization
selection, without test tuning. No final
outcome, event annotation, future sample, object ground truth, full integration
state, injected amplitude, noise vector, seed, or identity/hash is an input.
Commands may reveal applied target changes; the command-only ablation tests
whether measured response adds information beyond these commands.

For the full model, training/validation/test log losses are
0.2268/0.4065/0.3771 and failure AUROCs are 0.9806/0.8924/0.8523. At the
fixed threshold, the apple-B test flags 187/202 failures (92.6\% recall) and
22/58 successes (37.9\% false alarms among successful attempts), leaving 15
missed failures and 36 correctly unflagged successes. Commands alone flag
192/202 failures and 52/58 successes. The prevalence baseline uses
$p_{\mathrm{success}}=231/520$ and flags every attempt.

The measured-response model improves this comparison beyond command summaries.
The substantial success false-alarm rate limits usefulness at the fixed
threshold. Log loss and Brier score evaluate these probability forecasts;
they do not establish calibrated deployment failure rates. Test consists of
one previously inspected apple observation, both geometries appear in training,
and validation is cube-only. Therefore these results are exploratory and cannot
establish unseen-geometry transfer or population-level reliability.

Eventual outcome prediction is not equivalent
to forecasting loss within a future horizon. A later horizon-based task needs
supported acquisition, a separately versioned loss event, strictly causal
windows, and explicit handling of incomplete follow-up. Only four task-scored
drops occur in the entire collection. These metrics do not measure drop
detection, slip onset, or warning lead time; event recall at a fixed false-alarm
budget belongs to a later evaluation.

An illustrative CLI prediction for cube-A \path{episode_000534} gives failure
probability 0.4815 at 2.96 s, predicting success despite its recorded drop.
This attempt belongs to a training observation family and is not an additional
held-out result. It demonstrates a model error without implying a specialized
drop detector.

\section{Limitations and next work}
Two rigid geometries and four input families limit diversity despite the number
of attempts. The controller and embodiment are fixed, friction is nominal, and
each object's mass is held constant. Disturbances stress joint targets and are
frequently clipped; they are not a validated distribution of hardware error.
The task cutoff and failure precedence affect label meaning. Simulator contacts
and object truth provide useful supervision but are not tactile sensing or
automatically observable robot inputs. No online visual trajectory stream is
present. Cross-runtime reproducibility and physical contact fidelity remain
untested.

The first baseline is complete under the original task labels and grouped
development split. More untouched observation families, geometries, and physically
motivated conditions should follow before a broad generalization claim. Timed
post-acquisition loss requires additional supported events and an event-label
audit, rather than assigning every failed episode a slip time. External
VR-collected failures require licensing, embodiment, sensing, synchronization,
and outcome audits before use. Synthetic human-video generation remains an
exploratory idea with no implementation or demonstrated benefit.

Recovery is a separate future experiment. It needs matched continuation and
intervention outcomes, bounded actions and delays, and appropriate simulator
state restoration or repeated physical trials. A failure probability alone
does not establish recoverability. This working draft therefore makes no claim
that a predictor anticipates slips or improves recovery.
