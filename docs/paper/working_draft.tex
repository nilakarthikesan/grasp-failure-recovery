\author{Working draft: authorship and affiliations to be confirmed}
\date{12 October 2026}

\begin{abstract}
Simulation can generate labeled grasp attempts, but additional disturbed attempts
need not cover the conditions that cause prediction errors. We study a fixed
Shadow Hand grasp, lift and hold task using HUG proposals and MuJoCo execution.
A logistic outcome predictor trained from cube and apple closing histories
flags 187 of 202 failures and 22 of 58 successes in a retrospective development
test. A frozen evaluation on fresh objects exposes a mug executor collision.
After a separately versioned clearance correction, the unchanged predictor
warns on all 23 completed mug checks, including all 20 successes, across three
finger disturbance bounds. Both wrist commands in every successful mug lie
outside the original training ranges; reconstructed wrist terms push all 20
scores toward failure. This is an input and score diagnostic, not isolated
physical causation. A static check also finds no eligible mug configuration
among the eight wrist command pairs observed in training under the declared
clearance gate. Across eight subsequent frozen pairs of unfamiliar wrist
configurations, all 13 successes and three task failures trigger alerts.
Six pairs succeed at both settings, yet no warning clears. One changed task
label crosses the lift cutoff by a submillimeter amount. Wrist terms change
scores without establishing an improvement. We
specify a subsequent comparison of wrist coverage against
additional finger noise at matched training counts and class proportions.
That comparison remains unexecuted. Current results establish a reliability
problem and a constraint on its diagnosis, not improved transfer, slip timing
or recovery.
\end{abstract}

\section{Question and relation to prior work}
A useful grasp warning must distinguish a failed task from a successful attempt
in an unfamiliar configuration. Our research question is:
\begin{quote}
For a simulated dexterous hand, does training with more diverse successful
wrist configurations improve prediction of grasp, lift and hold outcomes on
unfamiliar objects and wrist configurations more than additional finger-command
noise, when both approaches use the same amount of training data, the same
success/failure proportions and the same model architecture?
\end{quote}
We distinguish the completed frozen-model diagnostic from the proposed training
comparison. The former motivates the question; it does not answer it.

Failure prediction and recovery are established topics. Gilday et al. combine
sensor-based grasp prediction with wrist-driven recovery and discuss errors on
novel objects using different successful grasp modes \cite{gilday}. FIPER
explicitly distinguishes benign unfamiliar observations from failures in
generative robot policies \cite{fiper}. SAFE studies multitask failure detection
\cite{safe}; SAFECAST investigates contrast-set training and calibration under
visual and language shifts, and reports that adding more perturbations does
not uniformly help \cite{safecast}. These results motivate controlled diversity
comparisons, not a claim that nuisance warnings or augmentation are new ideas.
Our candidate contribution is a reproducible, embodiment-specific investigation
of which simulated variations improve warnings at a matched training-data
budget. No new algorithm or real-world transfer result is claimed.

Pick-success models already test targeted data coverage: Amazon's Robin study
compares broader pick locations with an equal-sized collection
\cite{robin}. Vision and touch also support after-closing, before-lift outcome
prediction across diverse objects \cite{calandra}. These precedents motivate
checking measurements, calibration and baseline comparisons before attributing
our errors to wrist coverage. The proposed training comparison remains under
scope review; wrist-feature contributions alone do not demonstrate its remedy.

\section{Execution, labels and predictor}
\paragraph{Grasp source and simulation.}
HUG supplies grasp hypotheses from rendered RGB-D observations \cite{hug}.
Dex Retargeting maps the proposed human hand pose to an articulated right
Shadow Hand \cite{dex}; MuJoCo supplies physics \cite{mujoco}. We use HUG
proposals and selected development assets, not official benchmark grasp labels
or a HUG-Bench evaluation. A fixed sequence approaches, closes, settles, lifts
and holds through force-limited position servos. Physics steps are 2 ms;
recorded control intervals are 40 ms. We train an outcome predictor, not the
grasp controller. Initial RGB-D is saved; there is no online image stream among
the predictor inputs.

\paragraph{What noise means.}
The original \path{fixed_grasp_lift_v2} protocol adds an episode-constant
bias to 22 finger targets during designated closing and subsequent phases.
For a fixed draw $\boldsymbol\xi\sim\mathcal U([-1,1]^{22})$ and bound
$a\in\{0,0.15,0.60\}$ radians,
\[
 \mathbf u_t=\operatorname{clip}_{[\mathbf l,\mathbf h]}
 (\mathbf u_t^{\rm nominal}+a\boldsymbol\xi).
\]
The two wrist biases are zero. Requested offsets, clipped targets and measured
motion are distinct saved fields. This varies commands, not object identity,
obstacles, observation noise or a validated hardware error distribution.
Nonzero bounds share paired draws within a proposal and repetition. Noise can
improve or harm the grasp; the executed outcome determines its label.

\paragraph{Success and failure.}
A completed attempt has $y=1$ when the frozen scorer records qualifying contact,
peak lift at least 0.14 m, at least 2 s of consecutive HOLD samples above the
initial object center plus 0.12 m, final height loss at most 0.02 m, no approach
table collision and no timeout. Otherwise a completed task has $y=0$.
Setup errors and runtime clearance stops have null labels and remain outside
task-outcome metrics. Labels describe task completion, not physical slip onset;
threshold-adjacent acquisitions and category precedence are audited separately.

\paragraph{Causal inputs and fitting.}
Twenty closing samples from 2.20 through 2.96 s precede the first LIFT sample
at 3.00 s. Each contains 24 joint positions, 24 velocities and 24 applied targets.
Last value, mean, population standard deviation and last-minus-first change
produce 288 statistics; 269 are active under training-only normalization.
L2 logistic regression predicts success, with
$p_{\rm failure}=1-p_{\rm success}$ and an alert at $p_{\rm failure}\geq0.5$.
Validation log loss selects $\lambda=0.1$ from
$\{0.001,0.01,0.1,1\}$. Commands-only and training-prevalence baselines use
consistent splits. Outcomes, contacts, object truth, identities, injected noise
metadata and future samples are excluded from inputs. An alert is a model
prediction, not a physical failure or a controller intervention.

\section{Completed evidence}
\paragraph{Original pilot.}
Sixteen proposals in four observation families from a cube and a HUG apple
produce 1,040 attempts: 444 successes, 596 failures and no runtime errors.
Training, validation and test contain 520, 260 and 260 whole trajectories,
respectively, grouped by observation family. Both geometries occur in training;
the test is one reserved apple observation family. Splits were selected after
collection and inspection, so results are retrospective development evidence.
Failures comprise 590 acquisitions, two missing-contact tasks and four
scorer-defined drops, insufficient for a slip forecasting benchmark.

On this test, state plus command features attain failure AUROC 0.8523, versus
0.6419 for commands alone; log loss is 0.3771 and Brier score 0.1170.
At the fixed threshold the full model detects 187/202 failures, misses 15,
and falsely warns on 22/58 successes. The commands-only model warns on
192/202 failures and 52/58 successes. Better ranking than this ablation does
not establish reliable deployment or calibrated physical probabilities.

\paragraph{Fresh evaluation and executor correction.}
With model, normalization, threshold and v2 execution frozen, 2,080 new attempts
produce 659 successes and 1,421 failures. AUROC is 0.9173 on fresh cube/apple
scenes and 0.5588 on pooled new milk-carton/mug attempts. Geometry, mass and
failure composition change together; this is not an isolated object-shape
comparison. All 520 mugs collide during approach before the prediction cutoff,
so mug false alarms on successes cannot be estimated from this batch.

The Shadow forearm does not fit the original mug poses. Version
\path{fixed_grasp_lift_v3_table_clearance} changes wrist configuration and
compensates the carrier transform to preserve intended world palm and finger
geometry. Its nominal table clearance is 10 mm; a runtime guard checks an
actual 1 mm margin every physics step. This is a controller correction, not
prediction-triggered recovery. Checks planned before scoring contain 24 mugs
and three controls. Twenty-six finish without table contact; one high-noise
mug stops at about 2.858 s, before a full prediction prefix, and stays unscored.
The frozen model is then scored offline against all completed recordings.

All 23 mugs trigger alerts. Detecting 3/3 failures while warning on 20/20
successes is not useful discrimination at this threshold. Failure recall at
0.15 rad is undefined because no failures occur. These checks reuse controller
development cases; they are not an untouched benchmark. Independent arithmetic
reproduces all 26 model scores within $1.11\times10^{-16}$, with original
recordings and frozen model sources unchanged.

\paragraph{Wrist coverage and score attribution.}
Original training-only statistics exactly reproduce the saved normalization.
Both last wrist commands in all 20 successful mugs lie outside training ranges:
WRJ2 spans $[-0.2039,-0.1764]$ rad in training versus
$[-0.1464,+0.1745]$ in successful mugs; WRJ1 spans
$[-0.0394,-0.0146]$ versus $[-0.5312,-0.0817]$.
Net wrist terms reduce the success logit in every successful mug. Their share
of absolute weighted feature contributions has median 75.5\% and range
31.0\% to 86.9\%, excluding the intercept. This describes saved linear score
arithmetic, not a percentage of physical causation. Fingers also contribute;
object, proposals and controller changed together. Removing wrist features
or collecting more wrist data has not been shown to improve performance.

\paragraph{Static feasibility constraint.}
Before new rollouts, we check the eight distinct last wrist-command pairs
observed in the 520 training episodes against the eight declared mug proposals,
preserving intended world palm and finger geometry. None of the 64 combinations
meets the declared 10 mm nominal table-clearance gate; table clearances range
from $-65.211$ to $+0.187$ mm. All eight stored corrected postures revalidate
at 10.001 to 10.042 mm. These are static kinematic checks with physics stepping
blocked. They do not establish dynamic safety or rule out every point of a
continuous wrist range. A comparison using these stored training pairs cannot
honestly contrast familiar and unfamiliar safe mug wrist commands.

\paragraph{First frozen wrist pair.}
We freeze eight feasible pairs using geometry and training distance alone,
requiring at least 0.02 rad Euclidean command separation. A uses the nearest
eligible candidate in a deterministic bounded search; B retains the previously
corrected pose. Both settings remain outside training command ranges in every
selected case. The first eligible pair in the declared order is executed first,
with zero noise and separately versioned explicit v4 execution. It rejects
any substituted pose before motion and retains the existing runtime guard,
task parameters, scorer and predictor.

For this mug proposal, A commands $(0.113628,-0.202077)$ rad at the two wrist
joints and B $(0.099084,-0.226802)$ rad, a combined separation of 0.028686 rad.
Both tasks succeed, with peak lifts 0.146208 and 0.146077 m and the required
2 s hold. The frozen predictor warns on both: failure scores
0.9999999871 and 0.9999999785, with success logits $-18.169261$ and $-17.655064$.
The B-minus-A logit change is $+0.514197$, comprising $+0.529098$ in wrist terms
and $-0.014901$ elsewhere. A is slightly nearer the original command
distribution under the declared scaled distance, yet receives the higher
failure score. Training distance is not the learned decision function.

Applied finger command histories are identical. Nominal palm/finger paths
agree within $1.67\times10^{-16}$ m; actual maximum paired palm and finger
geometry differences are 0.05890 and 0.35723 mm. Independent replay reproduces
all saved states bitwise across 6,520 physics substeps, with no table contact.
The scoring report and execution declaration are preserved; a disclosed
auditor-only code amendment finalizes geometry and rounding checks after its
initial hash was declared. It changes no executed treatment or model input.
This both-success pair demonstrates persistent false warnings under a small
safe wrist change, not an improvement or a familiar-versus-unfamiliar contrast.
No failures occur in this initial pair, so its failure recall is undefined.
These v4 records remain separate from the earlier v3 checks.

\paragraph{Completion of the remaining declared pairs.}
The remaining seven pairs run once per posture without changing poses, noise,
model, normalization or threshold. All 14 complete: 11 successes and three
scorer-defined failed acquisitions, with no clearance stops. Every attempt
triggers an alert. Five new pairs succeed at both settings, one fails at both,
and one fails in A but succeeds in B. Including the preserved first pair gives
16 attempts, 13 successful false alarms and three detected failures; all six
both-success pairs warn at both settings. No alternative attempt is selected
after observing results.

The outcome-changing scene 003 seed 2 pair is cutoff-adjacent. A peaks at
0.139963979 m, only 0.036021 mm below the 0.14 m requirement, while B peaks at
0.140204587 m. Their peak lifts differ by 0.240608 mm; both satisfy qualifying
contact and the sampled 2 s HOLD measure. Original labels remain failure and
success. This crossing does not demonstrate slipping, dropping or a dramatic
recovery. The other two failed acquisitions peak at approximately 0.1304 and
0.1302 m. All three failures belong to two proposal families.

Manual probabilities match within $1.39\times10^{-17}$, original training
normalization reproduces bitwise, and independent replay reproduces all saved
states across 45,640 new physics substeps without table contact. Applied finger
commands and nominal world paths remain identical within each pair. Actual
closing-window palm and finger geometry differences reach 0.458 and 4.116 mm;
the largest occurs in the both-failed pair. These are not pure input
interventions or independent new object conditions.

At the fixed threshold the predictor behaves like an always-warn rule on these
cases. Descriptive failure AUROC is 0.9091 for the new batch and 0.8974 for all
16, based on only three related failures. This ranking signal does not repair
the operating-point false alarms, but also prevents concluding that the scores
contain no information. Threshold selection from these diagnostic outcomes
would not constitute fresh evaluation.

\section{Planned comparison and decision criteria}
\paragraph{Completed wrist diagnostic.}
The declared eight-pair diagnostic is now complete. Review the full inventory,
including changed outcomes, before choosing a training remedy. Compare
alerts, logits and wrist/nonwrist
terms in pairs where both actual tasks succeed; report all other outcome pairs
separately. Two out-of-range wrists test sensitivity, not familiar versus
unfamiliar coverage. No alert change may also reflect saturated probabilities.
This development diagnostic cannot demonstrate improvement to a frozen model.

\paragraph{Then test training diversity.}
Fit two new models with the same architecture and validation rule:
the original training base plus an additional finger-noise cohort, and the same
base plus a wrist-coverage cohort. Match selected episode counts, class
proportions and base object/proposal identities; fit normalization on each
training set only. Declare cohort selection and quotas before fitting, without
using prediction scores. Retain all generated, selected and excluded counts:
equal training-data budgets need not imply equal collection costs. If matched
cohorts cannot be formed, amend the design before fitting and retain the confound.

Declare one common fresh holdout of objects, postures and source families
outside training and validation; freeze both fitted models before scoring.
Also report the original frozen predictor as a reference, whose smaller
training budget does not itself isolate the diversity effect. Improvement
requires fewer false warnings among successful tasks without more missed
completed failures at the same 0.5 threshold on this common test. Report
numerators, denominators, object/posture breakdowns and uncertainty respecting
shared families. No failures means undefined failure recall; a few failures
cannot establish broad noninferiority. Neither warning on everything nor
clearing everything satisfies both objectives. No such comparison is completed.
A separately validation-fitted calibration reference would test whether coverage
training adds value beyond adjusting score calibration. Any mapping or threshold
must be fitted on separate validation data, with the current diagnostic result
preserved and the common fresh test untouched. No calibration is fitted here.

\section{Limitations and evidence availability}
Our completed diagnostic concerns one hand, fixed task definitions and reused
mug cases under a corrected executor. Simulation contact fidelity, real-world
transfer, usable warning lead time and prediction-driven recovery are untested.
The task target is eventual grasp, lift and hold completion, not impending
physical slip. Prior literature already studies nuisance warnings and diversity;
a distinct scientific contribution still requires a convincing controlled
comparison or informative negative finding. Static feasibility constrains that
comparison and should not be replaced by unsafe or mismatched trials.
